# game mode: what it measures

The game pass uses multi-person pose detection, a basketball-trained detector, motion and jersey appearance to keep track of people. It chooses a shooter from recent hand/ball contact and release evidence, then chooses the nearest plausible opposing contest. Persistent IDs make the pre-release comparison less random, but crowded frames, similar jerseys, tiny players, officials, and occlusion can still break identity.

The shot-space score is deliberately transparent:

```text
65% separation score + 35% contest-clearance score
```

Separation is projected hip-to-hip distance in shooter torso lengths. Contest clearance is projected ball-to-defender-wrist distance in the same units. Separation change is reported as context, not secretly folded into the grade. It compares the release separation with the median over 0.3–0.7s before release. It relies on persistent player identities, so it needs both players tracked on at least half the frames through release. Each earlier frame is rescaled by the camera's zoom, estimated from every player seen in both frames. Frames zoomed by more than about 20% are skipped. One player crouching or turning no longer voids it, and neither does a camera pan. Each defender hand is measured on the frame nearest release, within 0.1s, where both that hand and the ball are visible. A hand briefly hidden on the release frame therefore no longer turns the contest into a range, and the confidence drops with the time offset. Only a hand unseen for that whole window leaves the score as a range with "other hand unknown". The score is a 0–100 review heuristic, not make probability, expected points, or a professional player grade. Missing evidence stays missing; it does not become a zero.

### which shots game mode finds

Each game shot is labelled jump shot, floater, layup, dunk, "layup or dunk" or tip, and its evidence says why (`app/shots.py`).

- **Jump shots** still need a ball arc, raised-hand ball contact at release and a flight well above the release shoulders. When the release itself is hidden, the basketball detector's jump-shot class plus a supported arc also counts. The release time is then the last visible hand contact, and the evidence says so. The ball flies free from release to apex, so when a hand is still bending the ball's path after the estimated release (the estimate caught the gather or a pump fake), the release moves to that last contact before the apex.
- **Floaters** are arc shots where the detector's layup-dunk class is at least as confident as its jump-shot class around release, or whose ball reaches the basket 0.6–0.8 s after release (dev jump shots took 0.83 s or more; shorter flights are mostly a basket event fired early, so they say nothing). On a calibrated court the shot distance (the take-off spot, as for the distance metric) decides instead: an arc shot from under 10 ft is a floater, and a "floater" from over 16 ft is a jump shot. Rim finishes are not retyped by distance, since their distance is measured where the path passes the basket.
- **Arc shots that reach the basket within 0.4 s of release are rim finishes**, not jump shots or floaters. From broadcast height a layup off the glass still draws a small arc, so the arc path finds it. On the labeled clips, jump shots took 1.05–1.38 s to reach the basket and floaters 0.50–0.65 s. Such a finish is a tip when it comes within 1 s of the previous shot reaching the basket, and otherwise "layup or dunk": the contacts around the rim are too noisy on broadcast footage to tell the two apart. A tip is one touch, so a follow-up whose shooter had the ball for more than 0.3 s (contacts at most 0.3 s apart) or brought it more than 1.5 rim widths below the rim is a gathered putback, "layup or dunk". A single touch within 1 s of the previous shot getting there is a tip even when the ball takes up to 0.6 s to get back to the basket: nobody shoots a jump shot that soon after a rebound.
- **Layups, dunks, tips and putbacks** need a player's hand on the ball, then the ball reaching the basket within 1.2 s. "Reaching the basket" means a confident ball-in-basket detection, or the ball entering the rim's area when a rim exists. A contact is a wrist within 0.65 torso lengths of the ball, or within 0.9 when the ball is above a raised hand (on the fingertips, as in a tip at full stretch). The release is the last contact: when the ball enters the rim's area still in the hands (a dunk, a layup carried up), the same player's contacts up to 0.4 s later still count as the hold, and its end is the release. A tip is a raised-hand touch at the rim after the previous attempt got there, by a player who did not gather the ball (as above). Any other finish whose last contact is at the rim and reaches the basket within 0.35 s is "layup or dunk": on the labeled clips the contact's height against the basket did not tell them apart. A contact away from the rim whose ball takes longer than 0.4 s to get there, or any contact whose ball takes 0.6 s or more (in 2D a runner let go in front of the rim looks to be at it), is typed like an arc shot (floater or jump shot) instead. An arc shot released within 0.5 s of a rim attempt's contact is the same attempt, and the later release is kept. A rim attempt's make seen more than 0.8 s after the ball reached the basket is not credited, since a follow-up tip comes about that late. Without a rim or a basket detection, the detector's layup-dunk class plus the ball leaving the hands upward, above the head, gives "layup or dunk".
- **One attempt is one hand contact followed by the ball reaching the basket.** A ball rattling on the rim, or a rebound, has no new contact, so it belongs to the previous shot. A dribble has to leave the hands upward without bouncing. A pass that never reaches the basket is not a shot: when the ball of an arc is seen back below the rim, more than a rim width to its side, after the apex and before any basket event, the arc is dropped as a pass or lob. A blocked shot never reaches the basket either, so the arc stays when the detector's shot-block class fires confidently between release and apex. Without a rim in view this check cannot run. Two arcs whose first basket event is the same moment are one attempt. A ball-in-basket detection is ignored when the tracked ball is confidently seen elsewhere on all its frames (an empty net); when the ball is not seen confidently on those frames, the frames within 0.1 s are used instead, since the ball cannot get to the basket faster. An arc's rise has to be seen: the ball must be seen going up at least one of its radii between confident detections at most 0.1 s apart, so an arc drawn from stray detections across a lost ball (a net read as the ball, say) is dropped. A shot in flight claims its own arrival at the basket, so a hand touching it on the way (a contest, or a fan's hand the ball passes over in 2D) does not start a new attempt; once one player has held the ball for four sampled contacts, though, the flight is over. Rim attempts no longer test whether the ball stays on one parabola through the touch: a ball carried up to the rim or tipped moves as smoothly as one flying past a hand, and the test rejected most real finishes. A touch within 0.15 s of the previous shot reaching the basket is part of that arrival (the ball has to come off the rim before a follow-up), and a contact chain by the previous rim attempt's shooter that never let go of the ball is still that attempt. No attempt is released within 1 s of a make: the ball is falling through the net and has to be inbounded. Without a rim or a basket detection, the layup-dunk class needs a peak of 0.7 to call an attempt.
- **The shooter** for these attempts is the last player in unambiguous hand contact. It is checked against the decoded ball handler, and withheld when the two disagree (except for tips).
- **Ball-in-basket detections are never a make on their own.** Lone low-confidence detections also fired on an empty net. With a rim, the outcome uses the same rim-plane crossing and net-motion rules as jump shots. A ball-in-basket detection plus net motion can raise an otherwise-unknown rim attempt to "likely made".

**Scoring rim attempts.** Separation at the finish says little about a dunk, so layups, dunks and tips get a contest-only score: 100 × the contest-clearance component, measured at the last hand contact. Separation at the gather (the median over 0.3–0.7 s before that contact) is reported but not scored. These scores are labelled in the report and kept out of the mean shot-space score. Hidden-release jump shots use the normal score.

### how make/miss is called

The outcome comes from the ball's path at the rim (`rim_outcome` in `app/scoring.py`); net motion only adds confidence. On broadcast angles a ball passing in front of or behind the rim, a rim-out and a back-rim bounce all cross the rim's plane inside the rim in the image, so a crossing alone is no longer a make:

- **Made** needs the ball centre to cross the rim plane downward within 1 s of the arc's apex (labeled makes took 0.08–0.78 s), at least a quarter of the rim width in from either edge (a ball through the hoop is a ball radius, 0.26 rim widths, inside it; labeled makes crossed at 0.34–0.69), and no bounce back above the rim in the next 0.6 s.
- **Missed** is a crossing outside the rim; a crossing that comes back above the rim (two confident detections over the rim box within 0.6 s); a ball that comes down to the rim without crossing it and rises two rim-box heights, back above the rim; after no crossing on arrival, a later drop outside the rim; a crossing over the rim's edge after which the ball keeps falling beside the rim (every confident detection over the next 0.3 s, down to two rim-box heights below the rim, still over or beyond that edge, at least three of them: a ball through the hoop is held toward the rim's middle by the net); and an attempt followed by another one (a tip or putback) let go 0.5–2.5 s after it reached the basket and itself seen going through the rim, since a make ends the possession.
- **Unknown** is a crossing over the rim's edge whose fall is not seen, and a clean drop through the rim more than 1 s after the apex with no follow-up attempt seen: that is a rattle-in, a rebound or a putback, and the putback is its own attempt. Before this, a miss that was put back took the putback's crossing as its make.

The descent is followed from the top of the ball's path, so a dunk or a layup carried up, which goes through the rim before the hands leave the ball, is scored on its crossing. An arc shot is scored when a rim is known by the ball's arrival (within 1 s of the apex), not only at the apex: a camera following the ball, or the rim detector, can find the rim only as the ball comes down. A rim attempt's make is timed from the ball first seen in the rim's area, since the ball-in-basket class can fire on the net while the ball is still in flight. When the ball reaches the rim the detector can box ball and rim together as a second hoop; detections in one frame covering 30% of the smaller box are now one hoop, which had split the rim track and lost the rim just as the ball arrived.

On the dev labels (`eval/labels.csv`, 76 matched shots) the first version of these rules moved misses called made from 13 to 0 and misses called missed from 10 to 25, with makes called made unchanged at 39 of 45; outcome coverage went from 82% to 84%. The 0.25 edge margin is physically set but sits close to one labeled miss (crossing at 0.23), so treat it as lightly tuned.

With rim-attempt recall raised to 101 of 116 labeled shots, 22 matched shots were left unknown (10 makes, 12 misses). By the evidence they ended on: 8 had no crossing visible (4 dunks that went through the rim while still in the hands, 2 jump shots whose rim track split as the ball arrived, a dunk whose last contact was found after the make, a layup that never rose above the rim), 5 crossed over the rim's edge, 4 dropped through late, 3 were demoted by the follow-up rule (one because the ball-in-basket class fired 0.95 s before the ball got there), and 2 had no rim at the apex (one found on the descent, one never). The rules above raised coverage from 79/101 (78%) to 95/101 (94%), still 100% correct when called (no miss called made): makes 48 to 56 of 58, misses 31 to 39 of 43, over 16 different clips. The edge-fall rule fired on no labeled make's crossing, tested against either edge, for 2–4 detections and 0.2–0.5 s; three rim heights deep it caught one make whose ball swung the net aside. Applied to every dev shot regardless of its outcome, the follow-up rule flagged no labeled make for windows from 0–0.5 s up to 2–4 s. The hoop-box merge changed the rim track on 3 of 101 clips, the same for overlap thresholds 0.1–0.3; detection and shot type are unchanged. Six false shots that were unknown are now called missed by these rules; none is called made.

Still unknown (6): a dunk whose last contact was found after the ball went through (8628); a clip whose second half never shows a detected rim (d731); a missed tip followed by a tip-in that was not found as an attempt, whose crossing came 0.9 s after the first tip reached the basket, as late as a follow-up, so it is credited to neither (f267); a blocked shot whose ball was lost, with only low-confidence detections at the rim afterwards (76d9); a late drop over the rim's edge with too few detections below it (b34b); and a layup that never rose above the rim plane, where the ball-in-basket class fired once on a stray detection (cd04, so that class alone still cannot call a make).

Form mode reports 2D image-plane estimates such as release timing, launch angle, elbow angle, and upper-arm elevation. These are good for comparing your own reps from the same setup. They are not calibrated 3D biomechanics.
