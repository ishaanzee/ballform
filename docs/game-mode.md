# game mode: what it measures

The game pass uses multi-person pose detection, a basketball-trained detector, motion and jersey appearance to keep track of people. It chooses a shooter from recent hand/ball contact and release evidence, then chooses the nearest plausible opposing contest. Persistent IDs make the pre-release comparison less random, but crowded frames, similar jerseys, tiny players, officials, and occlusion can still break identity.

The shot-space score is deliberately transparent:

```text
65% separation score + 35% contest-clearance score
```

Separation is projected hip-to-hip distance in shooter torso lengths. Contest clearance is projected ball-to-defender-wrist distance in the same units. Separation change is reported as context, not secretly folded into the grade. It compares the release separation with the median over 0.3–0.7s before release. It relies on persistent player identities, so it needs both players tracked on at least half the frames through release. Each earlier frame is rescaled by the camera's zoom, estimated from every player seen in both frames. Frames zoomed by more than about 20% are skipped. One player crouching or turning no longer voids it, and neither does a camera pan. Each defender hand is measured on the frame nearest release, within 0.1s, where both that hand and the ball are visible. A hand briefly hidden on the release frame therefore no longer turns the contest into a range, and the confidence drops with the time offset. Only a hand unseen for that whole window leaves the score as a range with "other hand unknown". The score is a 0–100 review heuristic, not make probability, expected points, or a professional player grade. Missing evidence stays missing; it does not become a zero.

### which shots game mode finds

Each game shot is labelled jump shot, floater, layup, dunk, "layup or dunk" or tip, and its evidence says why (`app/shots.py`).

- **Jump shots** still need a ball arc, raised-hand ball contact at release and a flight well above the release shoulders: the apex at least 0.75 torso lengths above them, after the ball rose at least 0.55 torso lengths from the release contact (0.75 before 2026-10-15). The ball also has to get to a basket within 2 s of release (below). When the release itself is hidden, the basketball detector's jump-shot class plus a supported arc also counts. The release time is then the last visible hand contact, and the evidence says so. The ball flies free from release to apex, so when a hand is still bending the ball's path after the estimated release (the estimate caught the gather or a pump fake), the release moves to that last contact before the apex.
- **Floaters** are arc shots where the detector's layup-dunk class is at least as confident as its jump-shot class around release, or whose ball reaches the basket 0.6–0.8 s after release (dev jump shots took 0.83 s or more; shorter flights are mostly a basket event fired early, so they say nothing). On a calibrated court the shot distance (the take-off spot, as for the distance metric) and the shooter's run-up decide instead. An arc shot taken off within 4 ft of the point under the rim (the restricted area) is a finish, "layup or dunk", and is then scored and measured as one. One from up to 18 ft whose shooter came at the basket at 9 ft/s or more is a floater, since a jump shooter stops first; the approach compares the shooter's floor position 1–0.45 s before release with 0.35–0.05 s before (medians of the mapped feet). Otherwise an arc shot from under 10 ft is a floater, and a "floater" from over 16 ft is a jump shot. Rim finishes are not retyped by distance, since their distance is measured where the path passes the basket.
- **Arc shots that reach the basket within 0.4 s of release are rim finishes**, not jump shots or floaters. From broadcast height a layup off the glass still draws a small arc, so the arc path finds it. On the labeled clips, jump shots took 1.05–1.38 s to reach the basket and floaters 0.50–0.65 s. A ball that goes on to peak more than 1.5 rim widths above the rim has not got there when, in 2D, it crosses the rim's area on the way up or the ball-in-basket class fires early: its flight is timed from the apex to its first sighting in the rim's area (labeled finishes peaked at most 1.27 rim widths above the rim). Such a finish is a tip when it comes within 1 s of the previous shot reaching the basket, and otherwise "layup or dunk": the contacts around the rim are too noisy on broadcast footage to tell the two apart. A tip is one touch, so a follow-up whose shooter had the ball for more than 0.3 s (contacts at most 0.3 s apart) or brought it more than 1.5 rim widths below the rim is a gathered putback, "layup or dunk", and so is one whose ball fell more than 0.7 rim widths in the 0.3 s after leaving the hands: it was put straight down through the rim. A single touch within 1 s of the previous shot getting there is a tip even when the ball takes up to 0.6 s to get back to the basket: nobody shoots a jump shot that soon after a rebound. Arc shots are typed before the rim attempts are found, so each gets the tip test again once every earlier attempt's arrival is known.
- **Layups, dunks, tips and putbacks** need a player's hand on the ball, then the ball reaching the basket within 1.2 s. "Reaching the basket" means a confident ball-in-basket detection, or the ball entering the rim's area when a rim exists. A contact is a wrist within 0.65 torso lengths of the ball, or within 0.9 when the ball is above a raised hand (on the fingertips). By the rim (the ball within a rim width of the rim box) a touch at full stretch counts out to 1.25 torso lengths, since the pose model puts a fully extended arm's wrist short of the hand. That longer reach only finds the touch that starts a rim attempt: a hold or a gather still needs the ball in the hands, and a touch found only by it beside a ball another player was the decoded handler of in the last second is a contest. The release is the last contact: when the ball enters the rim's area still in the hands (a dunk, a layup carried up), the same player's contacts up to 0.4 s later still count as the hold, and its end is the release. A contact within 1.3 torso lengths of the basket is at the rim; one farther out has to send the ball up half a torso length. A tip is a raised-hand touch at the rim after the previous attempt got there, by a player who did not gather the ball or put it down through the rim (as above). Only a ball that was in the hands can be put down: a touch found only at full stretch is a tip however far the ball then falls. Any other finish whose last contact is at the rim and reaches the basket within 0.35 s is "layup or dunk": on the labeled clips the contact's height against the basket did not tell them apart. A contact away from the rim whose ball takes longer than 0.4 s to get there, or any contact whose ball takes 0.6 s or more (in 2D a runner let go in front of the rim looks to be at it), is typed like an arc shot (floater or jump shot) instead. An arc shot released within 0.5 s of a rim attempt's contact is the same attempt, and the later release is kept. A rim attempt's make seen more than 0.8 s after the ball reached the basket is not credited, since a follow-up tip comes about that late. Without a rim or a basket detection, the detector's layup-dunk class plus the ball leaving the hands upward, above the head, gives "layup or dunk".
- **One attempt is one hand contact followed by the ball reaching the basket.** A ball rattling on the rim, or a rebound, has no new contact, so it belongs to the previous shot. A dribble has to leave the hands upward without bouncing. A pass that never reaches the basket is not a shot: an arc shot, or a hidden-release jump shot, is kept only when its ball gets to a basket (a rim-area entry or a ball-in-basket detection) within 2 s of release, which a pass in transition with no rim in frame, or a ball batted around, never does. When the ball of an arc is seen back below the rim, more than a rim width to its side, after the apex and before any basket event, the arc is dropped as a pass or lob, unless the detector's shot-block class fired confidently between release and apex: a blocked shot can come down away from the rim and still get to the basket. Two arcs whose first basket event is the same moment are one attempt. A ball-in-basket detection is ignored when the tracked ball is confidently seen elsewhere on all its frames (an empty net); when the ball is not seen confidently on those frames, the frames within 0.1 s are used instead, since the ball cannot get to the basket faster. An arc's rise has to be seen: the ball must be seen going up at least one of its radii between confident detections at most 0.1 s apart, so an arc drawn from stray detections across a lost ball (a net read as the ball, say) is dropped. A shot in flight claims its own arrival at the basket, so a hand touching it on the way (a contest, or a fan's hand the ball passes over in 2D) does not start a new attempt; once one player has held the ball for four sampled contacts, though, the flight is over. Rim attempts no longer test whether the ball stays on one parabola through the touch: a ball carried up to the rim or tipped moves as smoothly as one flying past a hand, and the test rejected most real finishes. A touch within 0.15 s of the previous shot reaching the basket is part of that arrival (the ball has to come off the rim before a follow-up), and a contact chain by the previous rim attempt's shooter that never let go of the ball is still that attempt. No attempt is released within 1 s of a make: the ball is falling through the net and has to be inbounded. That holds for a basket taken off the shot before it (a held ball, under make/miss) too, except for the attempt that scored it: a dunk can still be in the hands as it goes through. Without a rim or a basket detection, the layup-dunk class needs a peak of 0.7 to call an attempt.
- **The shooter** for these attempts is the last player in unambiguous hand contact. It is checked against the decoded ball handler, and withheld when the two disagree, except for a one-touch follow-up at the rim (a tip, or a putback put straight down), which the decoded handler lags.
- **Ball-in-basket detections are never a make on their own.** Lone low-confidence detections also fired on an empty net. With a rim, the outcome uses the same rim-plane crossing and net-motion rules as jump shots. A ball-in-basket detection plus net motion can raise an otherwise-unknown rim attempt to "likely made".

**Scoring rim attempts.** Separation at the finish says little about a dunk, so layups, dunks and tips get a contest-only score: 100 × the contest-clearance component, measured at the last hand contact. Separation at the gather (the median over 0.3–0.7 s before that contact) is reported but not scored. These scores are labelled in the report and kept out of the mean shot-space score. Hidden-release jump shots use the normal score.

### how make/miss is called

The outcome comes from the ball's path at the rim (`rim_outcome` in `app/scoring.py`); net motion only adds confidence. Two checks across a shot's whole flight, a held ball and a ball that sat on the rim, are in `app/shots.py`. On broadcast angles a ball passing in front of or behind the rim, a rim-out and a back-rim bounce all cross the rim's plane inside the rim in the image, so a crossing alone is no longer a make:

- **Made** needs the ball centre to cross the rim plane downward within 1 s of the arc's apex (labeled makes took 0.08–0.78 s), at least a quarter of the rim width in from either edge (a ball through the hoop is a ball radius, 0.26 rim widths, inside it; labeled makes crossed at 0.34–0.69), no bounce back above the rim in the next 0.6 s, no sighting beside the net after the crossing, and no player holding the ball between the shot's flight to the basket and the crossing (both below). A jump shot's or floater's crossing also has to come within 0.25 s of the ball first coming down to the rim.
- **Missed** is a crossing outside the rim; a crossing that comes back above the rim (two confident detections over the rim box within 0.6 s); a ball that comes down to the rim without crossing it and rises two rim-box heights, back above the rim; after no crossing on arrival, a later drop outside the rim; a crossing over the rim's edge after which the ball keeps falling beside the rim (every confident detection over the next 0.3 s, down to two rim-box heights below the rim, still over or beyond that edge, at least three of them: a ball through the hoop is held toward the rim's middle by the net); an attempt followed by another one (a tip or putback) let go 0.5–2.5 s after it reached the basket and itself seen going through the rim, since a make ends the possession; a crossing inside the rim, on arrival or on a late drop, after which the ball is seen beyond either edge of the rim at net depth (two confident detections 0.5–2.2 rim-box heights below the rim's top within 0.3 s: a ball through the hoop stays in the net until it drops out of the bottom, about a rim width down); and a make whose ball one player held (four sampled contacts, caught rather than brushed) after the shot was on its way to the basket (its apex, or a rim attempt's release) and before the crossing. Nobody touches a shot on its way down to the rim, so that crossing was a putback's or a tip's; it still ends the possession.
- **Unknown** is a crossing over the rim's edge whose fall is not seen; a clean drop through the rim more than 1 s after the apex with no follow-up attempt seen and the ball not seen beside the net: that is a rattle-in, a rebound or a putback, and the putback is its own attempt (before this, a miss that was put back took the putback's crossing as its make); and a jump shot or floater whose ball sat on the rim more than 0.25 s (within a rim-box height above its top, after the apex) before it dropped through, since a rattle-in, a roll off the front of the rim and a tip-in look alike in 2D. Rim finishes are not timed that way: they roll off the fingertips onto the rim, and labeled finish makes sat there 0.37–0.68 s.

The descent is followed from the top of the ball's path, so a dunk or a layup carried up, which goes through the rim before the hands leave the ball, is scored on its crossing. An arc shot is scored when a rim is known by the ball's arrival (within 1 s of the apex), not only at the apex: a camera following the ball, or the rim detector, can find the rim only as the ball comes down. A rim attempt's make is timed from the ball first seen in the rim's area, since the ball-in-basket class can fire on the net while the ball is still in flight. When the ball reaches the rim the detector can box ball and rim together as a second hoop; detections in one frame covering 30% of the smaller box are now one hoop, which had split the rim track and lost the rim just as the ball arrived.
